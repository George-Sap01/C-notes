The wandering lantern drifted quietly along the forgotten avenue, where shadows bent themselves into awkward shapes and the pavement whispered about things that had never happened. A bicycle leaned against a wall of crumbling bricks, its spokes humming softly, as though they were keeping secrets from the passing wind. In that peculiar silence, every leaf seemed determined to invent a story, each gust of air weaving tales of imaginary kingdoms, painted oceans, and staircases that spiraled into nowhere. One could almost believe the road was alive, stretching itself lazily, yawning beneath the weight of invisible travelers. The doorways were tall, though no houses claimed them. Curtains swayed inside windows that lacked glass, suggesting families of echoes still lived there, eating meals of sound and drinking cups of dust. The sky overhead resembled an enormous page waiting for handwriting, and the clouds scribbled across it without caring about grammar or consistency. Some of them shaped themselves into creatures with impossible wings; others dissolved into careless smudges, like ink left too long in the rain. Beneath this shifting atmosphere, footsteps appeared without feet to make them. They clicked upon stones, paused, turned, and wandered again, never leading to anything except the certainty that something unseen shared the same pathway. Cats lounged upon rooftops, their eyes glowing like tiny fires, and they seemed to approve of the emptiness. Occasionally, one cat would yawn, and the gesture echoed louder than thunder. A bench sat beneath a crooked lamppost, and though no one occupied the seat, conversations hung in the air above it. Questions floated there, waiting for answers that would never arrive. "Why are the trees bending toward the south?" one asked. Another murmured, "What happens if the clock refuses to count tomorrow?" And the wind, restless and playful, offered no reply except laughter.Deeper in to the avenue, a fountain gushed without water. Its basin was empty, yet droplets of invisible liquid struck the stone with delicate rhythm, creating a melody only the most patient ear could pretend to hear. Birds perched nearby cocked their heads as though following along. The melody rose, sank, spiraled upward, and at last collapsed into silence. For a long while afterward, the silence itself rang. The more one walked, the less certain the direction became. North blended into south; east tripped into west. Street signs carried words that blurred when stared at too long. Sometimes they changed altogether: "Whispering Road" became "Forgotten Avenue," and then, with a sudden shiver of letters, "Memory Lane." Each name felt correct, yet none lasted long enough to be trusted. Shops lined the street but sold nothing comprehensible. A tailor’s window displayed garments woven from rain. A bookshop offered pages filled with letters that rearranged themselves each time they were read. One cover declared The Complete History of Tomorrow, while another muttered Recipes for Shadows. Passersby, if they could be called that, nodded solemnly, as though these selections were not unusual. At an intersection, a clock tower leaned precariously, its face showing not numbers but phases of a moon that never matched the one above. The bell inside struck every few minutes, though it never rang the same count twice. Seven beats followed by two, then eleven, then none at all. The sound startled the windows, made the cats blink, and stirred dust into small whirlwinds that danced across the cobblestones. Somewhere behind the buildings, a river moved without water. The channel carved itself across the earth, yet what flowed there was neither liquid nor stone. It was something softer, like memory, something heavier, like silence. Fishermen without lines sat on the banks, lowering empty hands into the current and pulling them back with smiles of triumph. They never caught anything, but their grins suggested the act of fishing mattered more than the catch. Time itself began to lose meaning in such a place. Day brightened into night, only for night to blink and scatter into dawn again. Stars appeared even when the sun stood high. Sometimes the stars rearranged themselves into sentences that nobody wished to read aloud. A passerby once whispered one of those constellations, and immediately the air shivered, the street shifted, and all the cats ran in the same direction. Since then, the stars wrote quietly, and the people pretended not to notice. And so the avenue went on, stretching farther than footsteps cared to follow, deeper than the eye could trust. Each corner held another impossibility, each window another story no one could finish. Yet strangely, it never felt lonely.
